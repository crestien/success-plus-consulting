As leaders, we spend a lot of time listening to our people. Part of the job is helping resolve conflicts, hearing complaints, and counseling employees through difficult situations. We want to be available. But without clear boundaries, a leader can spend most of the day mediating the same problems, and lose sight of the goals the team is there to reach.

Often, those problems trace back to one person.

## One person can change a whole team

A single source of gossip or negativity, whether in the office or on the production floor, can damage an entire workforce. It's tempting to ignore it and hope it goes away. It rarely does.

Negativity spreads. Once it takes hold, you start to see the cost: lower productivity, damaged morale, more conflict, higher turnover, and ultimately lost revenue. The good employees are often the first to leave, because they're the ones with options.

## Start by understanding the behavior

Before acting, take time to understand what's really going on. Is this person frustrated with something legitimate that's never been addressed? Are expectations unclear? Is something happening outside of work? Toxic behavior is sometimes a symptom of a bigger issue, and the fix isn't always what it first appears to be.

## Address it directly and privately

Once you understand the situation, don't wait. Sit down with the employee in private and:

- **Describe the specific behavior** and its impact on the team, without labels or personal attacks.
- **Listen to their side.** There may be context you don't have.
- **Set clear expectations** for what needs to change, and by when.
- **Document the conversation** and follow up as promised.

For more on running this kind of conversation well, see [How to Have the Talk](/blog/how-to-have-the-talk).

## Lead by example

Your team will take its cue from you. If you participate in gossip or let negativity slide, so will they. Hold everyone, including yourself, to the same standard: commitment to each other, to the customer, and to the goal.

Most people genuinely want to do good work. When leaders address problems early and fairly, they protect the team, and they often give the struggling employee the chance they need to turn things around.
