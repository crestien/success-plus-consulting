No matter how you slice it, turnover is a big deal. Every person who leaves takes their training, experience, and relationships with them, and the people who stay have to pick up the slack. If you're struggling with high turnover, you need a plan. That plan starts with understanding three facts.

## 1. Turnover costs more than you think

The obvious costs are recruiting, hiring, and training a replacement. The hidden costs are often larger: lost productivity while the position is open, overtime for the people covering it, mistakes made by new hires still learning the job, and the strain on morale when people keep walking out the door.

Most organizations never add it all up. When they do, the number is usually a wake-up call.

## 2. Turnover is a symptom, not the problem

Like a cough or a sore throat, turnover is usually a sign of something deeper. Pay and schedules matter, but they're rarely the whole story. People most often leave because of how they're led: unclear expectations, a lack of recognition, poor communication, or a supervisor who was never taught how to lead people.

If you only treat the symptom, with a signing bonus or a pay bump, the underlying problem stays put, and so does the turnover.

## 3. Your frontline leaders hold the key

The relationship between an employee and their direct supervisor has more influence on whether they stay than almost anything else. That means the most effective retention strategy is often developing the leaders closest to the work.

When supervisors set clear expectations, recognize good work, and treat people with respect, employees are far more likely to stay. When supervisors have a consistent way of leading, what we call leader standard work, those habits hold up even when things get busy.
