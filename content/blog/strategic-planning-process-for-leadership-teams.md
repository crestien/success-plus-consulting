**A strategic planning process should do three things: give the leadership team one clear direction, turn that direction into a few priorities with owners and measures, and plan for the people it will take to deliver them.** Most strategic plans don't fail because the ideas are bad. They fail because the plan sits in a binder, nobody owns it, and the organization doesn't have the leaders to carry it out.

Too many organizations try to chase a hundred ideas at once. Without focus, effort gets spread thin and people stop knowing what matters most. Here's the six-step process we use with leadership teams.

## Step 1: Assess where you are today

Start with an honest look at the organization: financial performance, customers, operations, culture, and leadership capacity. Include input from people at different levels, not just the executive team. The people closest to the work often see problems leaders miss.

Useful questions:

- What are we doing well that we must protect?
- Where are we falling short, and why?
- What's changing in our market, workforce, or industry?

## Step 2: Agree on where you're going

Define what success looks like in three to five years. Be specific enough that everyone on the leadership team could describe it the same way. If they can't, you don't have alignment yet.

## Step 3: Choose a few priorities

This is where most plans go wrong. Narrow your focus to three to five strategic priorities that will move you toward your goal. Everything else is either supporting work or a distraction. Saying no is part of strategy.

## Step 4: Assign owners and measures

Every priority needs one owner on the leadership team and a small number of measures that show whether it's working. Break each priority into short-term goals, typically for the next 90 days and the next 12 months.

## Step 5: Plan for the people

Strategy is carried out by people. For each priority, ask:

- Do we have the leaders to deliver this?
- What skills will our workforce need?
- Who is ready to step up, and who needs development?
- How will we lead people through the change?

This is where strategic planning connects to [succession planning](/solutions/strategic-consulting), [talent development](/talent-development), and change management. Skipping it is one of the most common reasons plans stall.

## Step 6: Review, adapt, and keep going

A strategic plan is a living guide, not a statue. Hold monthly or quarterly reviews where owners report progress against their measures, the team removes obstacles, and priorities are adjusted as conditions change. As we wrote in [The Basics of Strategic Planning](/blog/the-basics-of-strategic-planning), adapting is part of the plan.

## Common strategic planning mistakes

- Too many priorities, so nothing gets real focus
- Plans written by a few executives without input from the organization
- No owners or measures, so accountability is unclear
- Ignoring the people and leadership needed to execute
- Planning once a year and never revisiting the plan

## Why use a facilitator?

A skilled outside facilitator helps leadership teams have honest conversations, challenge assumptions, and reach real agreement. It also lets every leader participate fully instead of running the meeting.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does the strategic planning process take?

For most organizations, the initial process takes several weeks to a few months, including assessment, planning sessions, and finalizing priorities. The real work is the ongoing execution and review that follows.

### Who should be involved in strategic planning?

The leadership team should own the plan, but input from managers, supervisors, and employees makes it stronger and builds buy-in. Board members or owners should be involved in setting overall direction.

### How often should a strategic plan be updated?

Review progress monthly or quarterly, and revisit the overall plan at least once a year or whenever major changes occur in your market or organization.

### What's the difference between a strategic plan and a business plan?

A strategic plan sets long-term direction and priorities for the whole organization. A business plan typically focuses on the operational and financial details of a specific business, product, or period.
