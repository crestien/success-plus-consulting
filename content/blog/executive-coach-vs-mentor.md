**An executive coach helps a leader build specific skills and habits toward defined goals, while a mentor shares experience and perspective to guide a leader's broader growth.** Coaching tends to be structured and focused on performance; mentoring tends to be relationship-driven and longer term. Many leaders get the most value from a one-to-one advisor who can do both: hold them accountable for change and offer the wisdom of someone who has been there.

## What an executive coach does

A coach works with a leader on a defined set of goals, such as delegating more effectively, handling conflict, or leading a larger team. The coach asks questions, observes, gives honest feedback, and holds the leader accountable for practicing new behaviors.

Coaching works best when:

- There's a specific skill gap or performance goal
- The leader is stepping into a bigger role
- Feedback suggests a pattern that needs to change

## What a mentor does

A mentor draws on their own experience to offer guidance, perspective, and encouragement. Mentoring conversations often range widely, from handling a difficult situation to navigating a career decision.

Mentoring works best when:

- The leader is facing situations they haven't encountered before
- They need a trusted, confidential sounding board outside their chain of command
- The organization wants to build its next generation of leaders

## Coach vs. mentor at a glance

| | Coach | Mentor |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Specific skills and behaviors | Broad growth and perspective |
| Approach | Questions, feedback, accountability | Advice drawn from experience |
| Timeframe | Defined engagement | Often longer term |
| Best for | Performance goals, new roles | Judgment, confidence, career growth |

## Why many leaders need both

In practice, the line between coaching and mentoring blurs. A new plant manager might need help building specific habits, like running effective one-to-ones and holding supervisors accountable, while also wanting advice on navigating corporate politics or a tough personnel decision.

## Signs a leader would benefit from one-to-one support

- They're new to leadership, or new to a larger role
- They're facing a difficult employee, team, or decision
- They lack someone outside their reporting line to talk with candidly
- Feedback from their team suggests a blind spot
- You see high potential and want to prepare them for what's next

## What to look for in a coach or mentor

- **Real-world experience** in environments like yours
- **Honesty**, including willingness to challenge the leader
- **Confidentiality** and trust
- **Focus on results**, not just conversation
- **A personalized approach**, rather than a standard program

## Frequently asked questions

### Is executive coaching only for executives?

No. The same approach works for frontline supervisors, middle managers, and emerging leaders. In fact, investing in leaders early often has the greatest long-term payoff.

### How often should a leader meet with a coach or mentor?

Most leaders meet every two to four weeks, with more frequent contact during a transition or challenge. Consistency matters more than frequency.

### Can a mentor be someone inside the company?

Yes, and internal mentors can be valuable. An outside advisor adds objectivity and confidentiality, which makes it easier for leaders to discuss sensitive issues openly.

### How do you measure the success of coaching or mentoring?

Agree on goals at the start, then look for observable changes in how the leader behaves and the results their team produces. Feedback from the leader's manager and team is often the clearest signal.
